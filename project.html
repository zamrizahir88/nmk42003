Brief

The mini project lets your group apply what you learn in this course to a real IoT instrumentation system. You will select, build, program, calibrate and test a working prototype, and show that you can use engineering tools with precision.

Course outcomeCO3: Ability to operate engineering tools to implement instrumentation design in electronic system applications with precision
Level of complexityP4 (Mechanism): perform the tasks confidently and independently
Programme outcomePO5 Tool Usage: select and apply, and recognise the limitations of, appropriate techniques, resources and modern engineering and IT tools
Problem attributesSP3 Depth of analysis; SP7 Interdependence
Contribution15% of the final grade (a group assessment with an individual factor)

Groups

  • Each group has 3 to 4 students, from any lab section (groups of 3 only where the class numbers require it).
  • Every member takes part in all project activities and owns at least one part of the system: for example a sensor and its calibration, the condition logic, the actuators and hardware, or the WiFi and Bluetooth applications.
  • Register your group and submit your title proposal on this page by Sunday of week 8, 11:59 PM.

Your project must address ONE of these industries. You may propose your own title; every title must be approved by the lecturers. See the example titles.

Smart device integration, automated testing, product inspection and real-time fault detection in electronics manufacturing.

(b) Semiconductor industry

(c) Energy management

Tools

  • ESP32 with TUNIOT or the Arduino IDE.
  • MIT App Inventor (the Bluetooth application).
  • ThingSpeak, or an equivalent IoT platform such as Blynk (the WiFi dashboard and analytics).
  • A multimeter and other laboratory instruments for testing and calibration.

Other tools may be used if you can explain how they work and what their limitations are.

Design Requirements

All of these are compulsory. The proposal form on this page checks the first four before you can submit.

  1. Controller and standalone operation. Use an ESP32 as the main controller. The prototype must run on its own power supply as an all-in-one unit, without a PC connected during operation.
  2. Minimum hardware. Use at least TWO (2) sensors, at least one of which is analog and calibrated, and at least TWO (2) different types of actuator or output device (for example a relay with a fan or pump, a servo motor, a buzzer, an LED, an LCD).
  3. Three conditions. At least one sensor must define THREE (3) or more conditions (for example Normal, Warning and Critical). Each condition must produce a different system response, and at least two of these responses must use different actuators or actions (for example an alarm, switching or movement), not only different LED colours.
  4. WiFi. Send the data through WiFi to a cloud dashboard (ThingSpeak or an equivalent IoT platform such as Blynk) or to a webpage hosted by the ESP32. The dashboard must include analytics, for example a trend chart and the minimum, maximum or average value.
  5. Bluetooth. Provide a mobile application through Bluetooth (MIT App Inventor or equivalent) to display the system status and/or control the system.
  6. Local operation without WiFi. If WiFi is lost, the system must keep measuring and must still respond locally to the three conditions (for example the alarm or the relay still works). Readings may be stored and uploaded when WiFi returns.
  7. Sensor calibration. See Sensor Calibration.
  8. Limitations. State the limitations of the sensors, tools and platform you used (for example accuracy, range, WiFi dependence or the free-plan limits of the IoT platform).

Sensor Calibration

Compulsory. At least one sensor in your system must be calibrated, following the method in Chapter 7 (Transducer Calibration).

  1. Choose a known reference, for example a multimeter, a ruler or measuring tape, a protractor, known temperatures (such as ice water and room temperature), a known weight, known resistor values, or a reference sensor of stated accuracy.
  2. Record a calibration table of the sensor output (ADC count or voltage) against the reference value at no fewer than 5 points across the working range. Repeat each point to check repeatability.
  3. Obtain the calibration equation, for example a linear fit y = mx + c (a spreadsheet trendline or two end points), or a correction in code (offset and gain).
  4. Use the equation in the ESP32 program, so that readings are shown in real units (°C, V, A, cm and so on).
  5. Check and report. From your data, report at least TWO (2) of: sensitivity, repeatability, accuracy (the error at check points not used in the fit) or linearity. State the limitations of your calibration.
Good to knowThe equation or correction must come from your own measured data against a reference, not from a tutorial or datasheet alone. In the demonstration, you will be asked to show a reading next to the reference.

Technical Notes and Safety

Component Specifications

Know the specifications of every component you use, from its datasheet: for example the supply voltage, output range and accuracy of each sensor; the relay type (mechanical or solid-state), coil voltage and contact rating; the rating of the power supply or AC-to-DC adapter; current-limiting resistors for LEDs; and level shifting (a voltage divider or line buffer) for 5 V signals.

ESP32 Practical Notes

  • The ESP32 uses 3.3 V logic. Signals above 3.3 V (for example the HC-SR04 echo pin or an ACS712 output) must be reduced with a voltage divider or level shifter.
  • Use the ADC1 pins (GPIO32 to GPIO39) for analog sensors. The ADC2 pins cannot be used while WiFi is on.

Safety

  • Use low-voltage DC loads only (5 V or 12 V fans, pumps, lamps and LED strips).
  • Do not connect any 240 V mains wiring without the approval and supervision of the laboratory engineer.
  • Return borrowed laboratory components after the final demonstration. Groups are responsible for any damaged components.

Originality

Your design must be your own. Do not copy the reference projects shown in class or a complete project from the internet. Cite any code, libraries or designs you use from other sources in your report.
